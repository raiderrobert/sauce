# Color

Choosing and restraining color: accents, icons, neutrals and backgrounds, dark mode, semantic colors, contrast and palette building.

Practitioner heuristics; the user's scope, accessibility and the product's design system take precedence. **App UI** = product screens only, **Marketing** = landing pages and websites only, **From games** = a product-UI rule translated from a game-design principle (the principle is in the links file), untagged = both. *Note:* = uncited editorial caveat; follow it where it corrects a source number. Citations are `author/source locator` (a timestamp, a ¶ passage anchor, or ¶p and a PDF page); their links are in `references/links/color.md`.

## Restraint and purpose

- Reserve the accent for the primary action and active or selected states; don't let several accents compete on sibling cards. For a pop of accent on key data, see the rule below; for marketing pages, see the landing-page color rule below. Being the only accent-colored element is what makes it stand out. ([apple/design-foundations 17:21], [jain/color-mistakes 0:46], [jain/weirdly-perfect-ui 6:54], [nngroup/visual-design-principles 4:05], [schoger/transistor 11:49])
- Use color to communicate status or data meaning, not decoration; remove any color that is there just for color's sake. A reduced palette creates clarity. ([jain/color-mistakes 0:14], [jain/dashboard-flaws 1:50], [jain/every-ui-concept 5:41])
- Tone a color down slightly (less saturation or brightness) when it covers a large area or looks too rich beside a muted palette. The same color looks much brighter with more pixels and harsher on the eyes. ([kennedy/spendweek-redesign 5:42], [kennedy/wodify-redesign 6:56], [schoger/resolute 8:13])
- Keep UI icons (nav, buttons, inputs, list rows) neutral. Color an icon only when the color carries status or meaning, or for feature-illustration icons on marketing pages (e.g. in accent colors); on dashboards, add color through charts instead (see also data-and-dashboards.md). UI icons work as recognizable symbols; color there is noise. ([jain/color-mistakes 1:09], [jain/vibe-coding-cant-fix 2:43])
- Don't put the brand color on everything that can take it; give low-importance elements or cards a light tint of it with dark text. Full-strength brand color dominates the page and causes contrast problems. ([jain/color-mistakes 0:56], [jain/landing-page-levels 1:44])
- **App UI:** Use brand color on active states, such as the active tab or nav item, for a controlled pop of color. ([jain/color-mistakes 1:12], [jain/crucial-ui-elements 1:21])
- Spend a pop of the accent on the most important data, such as daily totals or key stats. Color draws attention to what matters and makes a utilitarian page less boring. *Note: Exception to reserving the accent for actions and active states: keep the pop to the one most important data type so it doesn't compete with the primary action.* ([kennedy/spendweek-redesign 9:02], [nngroup/principles-stripe 3:06])
- In product UI, skip 60-30-10 and stay almost entirely neutral with a tiny accent: e.g. Vercel's dark UI was roughly 90% black, 8% white and 2% red accent by screen area, as of 2026. Use 60-30-10 (dominant neutral, secondary, accent) on marketing pages. Product UI is a frame for content. *Note: The 90/8/2 example is a dark-mode UI; in light mode the large share is the light neutral.* ([jain/60-30-10-rule 0:08], [jain/color-mistakes 0:29])
- **Marketing:** On landing pages, let color come from product screenshots and curated imagery and keep buttons neutral (e.g. white on dark), bringing the bright accent in only on hover or a CTA reveal. A more vibrant, professional way to add color, and the accent stays tasteful rather than overpowering. ([jain/landing-page-levels 2:46], [jain/tesla-wannabe-redesign 4:15])

## Neutrals and backgrounds

- Tint neutrals (grays, borders, dark surfaces and dark-mode backgrounds) with a hint of the brand hue instead of pure gray or black, e.g. a bluish black, or a deep purple, red or green in dark mode; raise saturation as grays darken, keeping dark backgrounds around 20% brightness and at most 20% saturation. Cooler slate grays feel more technical. A pure or near-white page background is fine in light mode (see the light-mode layering rule); a tinted off-white (e.g. tan) adds personality. Integrates color without overpowering and makes the design feel cohesive. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 5:53], [jain/awful-to-amazing 5:04], [jain/color-mistakes 1:57], [jain/every-ui-concept 4:59], [jain/ui-cheat-codes 6:47], [jain/web-design-hacks 3:18], [kennedy/advanced-design-tips 4:35], [kennedy/spendweek-redesign 6:03], [kennedy/starting-a-ui-project 40:24], [schoger/transistor 2:33], [schoger/wp-pusher-checkout 9:07])
- Pick one gray family and use it everywhere, including product screenshots; with a warm primary such as orange, use true neutral grays instead of blue-tinted cool grays like Tailwind's default gray. Cool grays don't work as well with warm primaries, and a mismatched gray family (e.g. slate beside neutral screenshots) stands out. *Note: Exception to the tinting rule above: tint grays toward a cool brand hue, but keep them neutral beside a warm primary.* ([schoger/bad-about 12:02], [schoger/dashboard-with-ai 0:05:00], [schoger/designing-with-claude-code 0:05:07])
- Keep the page background plain: almost never bright colors or gradients across the whole page. Put any gradient on a hero or section background and use shades of one hue; the exception is a dark-mode section background gradient of two brand colors darkened way down (see also the gradient rules in depth-and-polish.md). Backgrounds should stay in the background; mixed-hue gradients like blue-green rarely work. *Note: The hero-or-section scope is editorial; the sources' gradients each sit behind a single section.* ([jain/beginner-mistakes 1:23], [jain/color-mistakes 1:40], [jain/google-ai-redesign 2:58])
- Plan neutrals up front and pick only from that set: a fixed scale of 9-10 grays covers light and dark mode; use 3-5 for a landing page; for product UI about four background layers, one or two stroke colors, and about three text grays (primary text, secondary text, icons) (see also the text-tier rule in typography.md). Many ad-hoc grays look messy in design and code. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 0:40], [schoger/bad-about 11:35], [schoger/transistor 3:05])
- **App UI:** In light mode, pick one layering scheme: an off-white page with white cards, or a white page with slightly darker cards. Keep the page at 98-100% white and the app frame or sidebar only very slightly darker (e.g. Mercury adds 2% blue to its sidebar). Large surfaces need only a slight shift; Linear, Notion and Vercel sat at 98-100% white as of 2026. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 0:29], [jain/color-mistakes 1:48])

## Gray text and icons

- Make gray text and borders solid gray values, not black at reduced opacity; hand-pick de-emphasized text colors in an HSB picker. Transparent grays look different depending on the background they sit on. ([schoger/transistor 2:45], [schoger/tuple 8:53])
- On colored or image backgrounds, derive elements from the background: dividers as white at about 20% opacity, low-opacity borders, off-white fills in the background's hue, and secondary text hue-shifted about 10°. Solid colors clash with background visuals and look muddy; hue-matched tints keep the result rich and vibrant. *Note: Exception to the solid-gray rule, for surfaces where the background should show through. Check secondary text still meets 4.5:1 on the colored background.* ([kennedy/advanced-ui-tips 10:41], [kennedy/wodify-redesign 5:15], [schoger/dashboard-with-ai 0:09:15], [schoger/tuple 9:06])
- To find a secondary text shade, lower the text color's opacity: about 60% for secondary labels, about 70% for a secondary decorative element such as a pipe separator. *Note: Sources disagree with the solid-gray rule: opacity is fine on one known surface; when the text sits on varied backgrounds, bake the result into a solid value.* ([kennedy/advanced-design-tips 5:53], [kennedy/timezones-redesign 5:53])
- Give inactive icons a softer color than their labels, e.g. 60% opacity against 70% for text, or gray-500; keep the active icon at full strength (e.g. white, or the theme color). Icons cover more pixels, so the same gray reads heavier than the text beside it. *Note: Icons that are the only cue still need 3:1 against their background (WCAG 1.4.11).* ([kennedy/spendweek-redesign 7:28], [schoger/dashboard-with-ai 0:54:34])

## Dark mode

- **App UI:** Create depth in dark mode with lighter raised surfaces and borders: each elevation step gets a lighter fill. Shadows barely read on dark backgrounds; if you use one, make it stronger than in light mode. Surfaces lighten as they elevate; a light-mode shadow disappears against a dark page. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 4:14], [jain/color-mistakes 1:52], [jain/every-ui-concept 5:57], [jain/ui-cheat-codes 7:29], [jain/web-design-hacks 6:14])
- Space dark-mode neutrals about twice as far apart as light mode: about 4-6% white between adjacent layers versus about 2% (per layer, not total). In HSB, raise brightness about 4-6 per layer and lower saturation only for a saturated base (source: 10-20 per layer from a saturated dark blue; lightly tinted neutrals need little or none). Dark colors look more alike, so they need more distance. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 3:29], [jain/color-mistakes 5:03], [jain/macos-apps 5:05], [jain/ui-cheat-codes 7:36])
- Use the 300-400 step of the accent ramp as the dark-mode primary (hover at 400-500), with dark text on fills that light, and lighten over-saturated colors; e.g. with Tailwind scales, light mode = 50 background + 500 for non-text accents, dark mode = 950 background + 300 primary (source: light mode 50 + 500 accent). Primary colors are usually far too saturated on dark backgrounds. *Note: In light mode, links and white-text fills need the step that passes 4.5:1 (see the ramp rule below).* ([jain/60-30-10-rule 3:56], [jain/awful-to-amazing 4:37], [jain/ui-cheat-codes 7:07])
- Brighten borders and surfaces enough that layers stay distinguishable, but lower border contrast when light borders look harsh against dark cards. Dark layers need bigger differences, yet bright outlines glare. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 4:03], [jain/color-mistakes 5:03], [jain/every-ui-concept 5:52])
- Don't build dark mode by inverting light mode; design both as distinct but consistent palettes, tuning each color separately for light, dark and increased-contrast appearances. Inversion gives light mode too much contrast and dark mode too little separation. ([apple/explore-the-new-design 0:11], [jain/color-mistakes 4:55], [jain/macos-apps 5:05])
- Dim text in dark mode: use light grays for most text and reserve pure white for the most important text and actions. Dark mode is about reducing eye strain. *Note: Editorial: e.g. secondary text as white at about 60-70% opacity; check 4.5:1 against the actual surface.* ([jain/60-30-10-rule 4:03], [jain/color-mistakes 4:37])
- Desaturate saturated elements such as logos and chips in dark mode; reduce chip saturation and brightness and switch to brighter chip text. Bright chips are harsh on dark surfaces. ([jain/color-mistakes 5:26], [jain/every-ui-concept 6:04])

## Semantic and status colors

- Color destructive actions such as delete red, never the brand color, and keep semantic colors even in a neutral or monochrome palette: green for success, red for danger or urgency, yellow for warning, blue for trust. Brand color draws attention without signaling destruction; semantic colors are signifiers, so they override a monochrome palette. *Note: Don't rely on color alone; pair it with an icon or text label (WCAG 1.4.1).* ([jain/60-30-10-rule 4:35], [jain/color-mistakes 5:51], [jain/dashboard-flaws 1:58], [jain/every-ui-concept 5:30])
- Give semantic colors a slight twist, e.g. rose or pink for high severity instead of default red and a lighter amber, but keep status hues clearly distinct (lime reads too close to warning yellow). A slight twist on plain red and green adds character; too-similar hues stop telling statuses apart. *Note: Stay within the expected hue family so the meaning still reads, and pair color with an icon or text (WCAG 1.4.1).* ([schoger/dashboard-with-ai 0:29:47])
- If the brand color is red or yellow, a near-black dark gray from the palette works for primary buttons instead. Red and yellow are associated with warnings and destructive actions. *Note: Exception to reserving the brand accent for the primary action.* ([schoger/transistor 12:07])
- **App UI:** When removing a colored alert container, keep the severity with bold red text plus a small red dot beside it. Red text alone lacks the impact the red box had. ([schoger/resolute 5:08])
- **App UI:** Notification badges may use the brand color instead of red. Notifications are far more flexible than destructive actions. ([jain/color-mistakes 6:03])

## Contrast

- If a brand color fails WCAG with white text (check sampled brand colors and greens especially), darken it, shifting hue toward the brand, or switch to a passing complementary color; use a darker brand shade for links. Adapt brand colors for UI rather than using them raw. Many brands pair a primary with an adapted secondary for exactly this reason. ([jain/color-mistakes 3:06], [schoger/resolute 7:20], [schoger/transistor 11:47], [schoger/wp-pusher-checkout 8:16])
- Colored pairs fail contrast most often on chips, icons on tinted backgrounds and tab labels: check those pairs and darken the label or icon until it passes. The whole point of color is that people can see it. *Note: WCAG AA requires 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text and UI graphics; compute the ratio with a tool rather than eyeballing it.* ([jain/color-mistakes 0:23], [jain/crucial-ui-elements 0:49], [jain/vibe-coding-pro-ui 5:38])
- When white text on a color fails, or darkening it turns muddy (orange goes brown), invert: a lighter version or soft tint of the hue as background with a saturated dark shade of that hue for text or icons, not gray. Use this for alerts and status badges. Dark-on-light passes contrast more easily, and same-hue text feels glued to its container. ([schoger/bad-about 7:34], [schoger/transistor 13:39])
- Keep text over photos legible: put a black layer at about 70% opacity behind it, or desaturate the photo and overlay a bold brand color with the multiply blend mode. Gives foreground elements higher contrast against the photo. *Note: Check contrast against the lightest part of the image behind the text.* ([kennedy/starting-a-ui-project 37:47], [schoger/wss 1:42])
- With a Tailwind-style gray scale, the source treats grays 600-900 as accessible for text on white and 100-600 for text on black. Those ranges were accessible on those backgrounds in the source's palette. *Note: For accessibility, with Tailwind's current default gray, 500 is about 4.3:1 and 600 about 2.8:1 on pure black, both under 4.5:1, so use 100-400 there; check your own scale against the actual surface.* ([schoger/transistor 3:31])

## Palettes and scales

- Start the palette from one base color (from the brand or its key imagery, e.g. a slate green instead of default blue) and extend it with slightly rotated analogous hues and one complementary color, not opacity variations alone. Opacity variations alone feel bland. ([jain/color-mistakes 2:49], [jain/every-ui-concept 4:59], [jain/ui-cheat-codes 3:27], [jain/vibe-coding-cant-fix 2:06], [kennedy/starting-a-ui-project 38:45])
- Build the accent as a 50-950 ramp rather than one color: use the 500 step for non-text accents, the first step that passes 4.5:1 on white (often 600-700 for blues, darker for greens and yellows) for links and white-text fills, and a darker adjacent step for hover, with light tints for chips and backgrounds (source: 500 or 600 main, 700 hover, 400 or 500 links). Take hover and pressed colors from adjacent steps (see also components-and-states.md). Large design systems build components from ramps. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 2:19], [jain/every-ui-concept 5:14])
- Don't accept indigo as the default accent; choose a deliberate brand color or a neutral palette (e.g. gray-950 as the primary). AI tools default to indigo every time, so it reads as generated. ([schoger/dashboard-with-ai 0:02:31], [schoger/designing-with-claude-code 0:03:18])
- Reference colors through semantic tokens named by purpose (label, secondary label, secondary background), not appearance, for text, backgrounds and icons. They adapt automatically across light and dark modes, contrast settings and environments. *Note: On the web, e.g. CSS custom properties such as --color-label redefined per theme and for prefers-contrast.* ([apple/design-foundations 17:06], [apple/principles-of-great-design 14:46])
- Derive a few darker shades from a base color in HSB, per shade: +20 saturation, -10 brightness, and hue ~20° toward blue (the source derives two shades this way). Shifting toward blue matches perceived darkness: blues and purples read darkest, yellows lightest. *Note: Values are per step; across a full 50-950 ramp, shrink the hue shift so the total stays small.* ([jain/ui-cheat-codes 3:20])
- To darken or separate similar light warm backgrounds such as tans, raise saturation and shift hue (e.g. toward red) instead of only lowering brightness. Lowering brightness alone makes tans muddy. *Note: Complements the toward-blue shade rule above, which derives darker shades of a base color rather than light warm surfaces.* ([kennedy/advanced-design-tips 4:03])
- Theme neutrals in OKLCH: from a gray, drop lightness by ~0.03, raise chroma by ~0.02, then set the hue to the brand's. Consistently themes any design, including dark mode. ([jain/60-30-10-rule 5:53])
- Match the palette to brand personality: clean, simple brands use bright primary hues or one hue with variations; luxury or formal brands use grayscale with possible gold accents; quirky brands use muted hues (an orangish rather than pinkish red). ([kennedy/starting-a-ui-project 26:36])
