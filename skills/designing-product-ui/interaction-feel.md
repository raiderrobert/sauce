# Interaction feel

How individual actions feel: acknowledgement, speed, keyboard feedback, waits, proportional feedback and delight, and when to hold back.

- No motion means no movement or scale; a color change, a single blink and a short opacity fade are allowed.
- No motion: command menu, context menu, keyboard navigation and selection, row selection, repeated tooltips, typing.
- Motion OK: modals, drawers, sheets, toasts, page and tab switches, completions, first-run moments.
- Trigger wins: anything opened or changed by a keyboard shortcut appears without movement, even a modal. The surface lists apply to pointer and touch.
- Feedback ladder, bottom to top: keyboard and very frequent actions get a state change only, no motion; routine clicks get one small cue on the affected element; completions users care about get a clear confirmation; rare milestones may get layered feedback.
- Pro tools use the first three rungs and skip layered feedback; consumer and mobile apps may use all four.

Practitioner heuristics; the user's scope, accessibility and the product's design system take precedence. **App UI** = product screens only, **Marketing** = landing pages and websites only, **From games** = a product-UI rule translated from the cited game-design Source principle, untagged = both. *Note:* = uncited editorial caveat; follow it where it corrects a source number. Citations are `author/source locator` (a timestamp, a ¶ passage anchor, or ¶p and a PDF page); their links are in `references/links/interaction-feel.md`.

## Acknowledge every action

- Give every action on a control an immediate visible response, whether by tap, click or shortcut: a pressed state, a progress label ('Processing your order') if it runs long (see also the wait rule in Waits, loading and progress), and a confirmation when done (a 'Done' label or a 'Copied' chip). Keyboard shortcuts get this feedback without motion (see Keyboard and power-user feedback). Without a response the app seems to ignore the user, and a slow action looks like the click didn't register. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-important-for-an], [apple/touch-on-mac 10:56], [jain/beginner-mistakes 5:39], [jain/every-ui-concept 7:31], [jain/macos-apps 6:46], [jain/tiktok-ux 4:06], [saffer/microinteractions 27:45], [vohra/beyond-gamification 8:29])
- **From games:** When a command the app defines (a shortcut, submit, undo) can't run, or a disabled control is used, give a specific, friendly response, never silence. Users learn the system's limits from its responses; silence reads as the app being broken. Source principle: Make misses and off-script attempts produce something: props break when shots miss, and text games reply in fun, unexpected ways to things players aren't supposed to try. *Note: At list boundaries, such as arrowing past the last row, stay silent or show a non-interrupting inline cue; never stack repeated messages. For a disabled control, say why it's disabled and how to proceed (see also the disabled-button rules in components-and-states.md).* ([jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:15], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 14:27])
- **App UI:** Don't let content change silently after an action the app took on the user's behalf (auto-categorize, AI edit); at minimum show a toast, ideally animate the changed content itself, such as a brief highlight in the accent. If users blink they miss it and can't feel the feature working. *Note: Announce the change through a polite live region too; the animation alone is invisible to screen-reader users.* ([raroque/apps-feel-premium 0:53])
- **App UI:** Communicate invisible rules through feedback as users act, escalating near a limit: MailChimp's chimp arm stretches, then pops off, as a newsletter grows too long. Rules are invisible; feedback is the only way users understand them. ([saffer/microinteractions 25:18])
- **From games:** Put a cue where the user is looking when a change happens elsewhere, and show hidden state such as syncing or a failed save. Users, like players, miss changes outside their focus, and invisible state looks like nothing is happening. Source principle: Grab attention with a particle, sound or light before a scripted event, since players look in the wrong place; soldiers call out cover and grenades to broadcast hidden AI state. *Note: For accessibility, announce important status changes in a polite live region.* ([valve/half-life-documentary 0:14:14])
- **From games:** In checklists and task lists, keep a completed item visibly checked until the next visit; in triage queues, remove it. Where completion is progress, permanence shows users what they accomplished. Source principle: Leave bodies, blood, decals and shell casings in place so players see the aftermath of their actions, and respond to accumulated actions, like marines running away. *Note: In a triage queue, open the next item instead (see the one-item-at-a-time rule in Speed and flow).* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 4:21], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 13:07], [valve/half-life-documentary 0:16:36])

## Speed and flow

- Respond the instant an interaction starts: highlight a control on touch down, and show the pressed state or optimistic result before any network call, aiming for under 100ms to first visual response. Remove latencies and timers that crept in during design. People are very sensitive to latency; slower responses invite distraction and break flow, and across hundreds of interactions a day small delays add up. *Note: For an optimistic result, show the change as done and roll it back with an error if the request fails (see also the optimistic-update rule in components-and-states.md).* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-not-just-swipes], [apple/principles-of-great-design 14:08], [eldh/invisible-details ¶some-product-and-engineering], [slack/incremental-boot ¶loading-one-second-faster], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶superhuman-browser-app-is], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶milliseconds-matter-they-may])
- **App UI:** Show search results as soon as typing starts: query local cache and server at once, merge live, and stage inserts to avoid pop-in. A separate 'search the server' step pushes users to switch tasks while waiting. *Note: Announce result counts politely to screen readers and don't move focus as results merge.* ([apple/intuitive-search 11:50], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶it-is-also-simultaneously])
- **App UI:** Make the next action obvious in sequential triage queues (an email inbox, a review queue): show one item at a time without the list beside it, and when the user finishes one, e.g. archives an email, open the next instead of returning to the list. Seeing what's next makes users distractible, and returning to the list forces a decision every time; hesitation kills flow. *Note: Outside a queue, e.g. after closing a single issue, keep the user where they were. Where users compare or navigate entries, a split view still fits (see also the search-placement rule in layout-and-hierarchy.md).* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 11:38], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶their-next-action-should])
- **App UI:** Pair a double-tap or double-click with a single action only when the single action is harmless and immediate, like select (single selects, double opens); otherwise avoid double actions. A competing single action must wait about half a second to see whether a second tap follows; a selection is correct either way. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶tapping-to-show-the], [apple/touch-on-mac 3:10], [jain/software-must-lie 1:11])

## Keyboard and power-user feedback

- **App UI:** Give anything opened or changed by a keyboard shortcut, even a modal, and overlays opened hundreds of times a day (command menus, app switchers, keyboard navigation and selection, row selection) feedback but no motion, as defined in the intro. At that frequency even snappy motion feels slow and disconnected; mouse and keyboard interactions can often skip motion without feeling jarring. *Note: A shortcut still needs a visible result (see Acknowledge every action). Context menus also appear instantly, while a pointer-opened dropdown or popover may grow from its trigger in about 120–180ms (see also the menu-motion rule in motion.md).* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶if-we-for-a], [kowalski/great-animations ¶a-good-tip-here], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶if-it-animated-every])
- **App UI:** If the product has a command menu or the user asks for it, open it with Cmd/Ctrl+K, let users type to find any action and see its shortcut, and make every action reachable from the command menu or a shortcut, with single-key shortcuts for frequent ones; all controls stay keyboard-operable. Users knew what they wanted but forgot how; the palette teaches the shortcut for next time. *Note: Don't let single-key shortcuts fire while typing in a field, and let users turn off or remap them (WCAG 2.1.4).* ([eldh/invisible-details ¶you-can-take-almost], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶hundred-milliseconds-response-times], [vohra/beyond-gamification 13:10])
- **App UI:** Never drop or misroute fast input: buffer keystrokes so text typed straight after a shortcut, like C then a name, lands in the new field. Ignored inputs feel like a fighting game ignoring half your moves. ([vohra/beyond-gamification 8:02])
- **App UI:** On menu selection, briefly blink the chosen item in the accent color, then subtly fade the menu out. Fire the action immediately; the blink never delays it. The blink assures the selection succeeded; the fade is more graceful than vanishing. *Note: Keep it to a single short blink, well under three flashes per second.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶the-selected-item-briefly])
- **App UI:** Make inputs forgiving: accept loose text like '3d' or '8am in Tokyo' and do your best to interpret it. Forgiving inputs invite playful exploration and pleasant surprise. *Note: Echo the interpreted value (e.g. the resolved date) so users can confirm it.* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 9:07])

## Waits, loading and progress

- Pick wait feedback by duration (thresholds in the note). Instant: no indicator. Short: show the pressed or optimistic state, then the result; don't flash a spinner. Longer: a spinner or skeleton where the content will appear (a faster-spinning spinner also feels quicker). Long or multi-step: a progress bar or step labels. Show the work itself (each source searched, each criterion matched, results as found) only for operations users value for the effort, such as search aggregation, AI generation or analysis; everywhere else speed wins: never add delay to show effort. An indicator flashed on a fast response reads as lag, while longer waits need visible progress; seeing the work raises the perceived value of a result users value. *Note: Editorial thresholds: instant = under about 100ms; short = about 100ms to 1s; longer = over about 1s. Duration is usually unknown: show the pressed state at once, show the spinner or skeleton only if still pending after the short threshold, and once shown keep it about 300ms so it doesn't flash. Pair any spinner with text or a polite live-region status, and throttle a running tally so screen readers aren't flooded.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2], [kowalski/great-animations ¶fast-animations-improve-the], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶a-fasterspinning-spinner-makes])
- Use what's already on screen for feedback: turn the pressed submit button into the progress indicator and disable it until the action completes. It confirms the click, avoids adding a progress bar and prevents repeated attempts. *Note: Prefer aria-disabled plus aria-busy so focus isn't lost, and announce completion.* ([nngroup/disabled-buttons 2:31], [saffer/microinteractions 27:09])
- **App UI:** Make progress indicators carry real information: keep a bar for remaining time, show task counts on the trigger like Chrome downloads, match spinner segments to running processes. Reliable information about the wait reduces its cost, and users don't have to go look. ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p3], [saffer/microinteractions 19:19])
- **App UI:** Fill unavoidable waits with loading messages or a little humor instead of a blank screen, as a stopgap, and still minimize time spent on them. It eases idle time and keeps the wait from feeling stalled, but getting users back to their work matters most. *Note: Keep loader animation subtle and skip it under prefers-reduced-motion.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2], [saffer/microinteractions 9:23], [slack/easter-eggs ¶the-custom-loading-messages], [slack/incremental-boot ¶seeing-these-messages-is])
- **App UI:** Keep show-the-work waits short and cut long ones: the benefit of showing work declined after ~30 s for flight search and ~15 s for dating search. Value drops as waits lengthen, and delays that look avoidable cause uncertainty and anger. *Note: Calibrate to what users expect from comparable services.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p14])
- **App UI:** Prefer showing real operations over fabricated busywork, and don't spotlight effort when the result is likely poor. Visible effort followed by a bad result scored worst of all, as users blame the service; faking labor nears an ethical line and backfires if users notice. ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p12])

## Proportional feedback

- **From games:** Show an action's result on the affected element itself, e.g. a brief flash or pulse that fades back, so the change is unmistakable. Feedback on the changed element shows exactly what the input did. Source principle: Make it unmistakable that a hit landed, on the thing hit: impact effects, a hit animation, knockback, blocks reacting; aim for maximum output for minimum input. *Note: Routine actions get one such cue; layering several effects is for completions users care about (see the layered-feedback rule in this section and the ladder in the intro). Keep it to one short pulse, well under three flashes per second.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 3:10], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:24], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 11:43])
- **App UI:** Layer feedback only on completions users care about and rare milestones, e.g. after sending a campaign: the send button turns into a check, the background expands and a short message springs in. Routine actions keep one small cue. Matching the emotion of a real accomplishment is what people remember; repeated on routine actions, layering becomes noise. *Note: Follow the ladder and product-type line in the intro: pro tools skip layered feedback. Respect prefers-reduced-motion, keep layered effects short so they never delay the next action, keep any sound optional, and skip tacked-on confetti.* ([nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶subtle-animations-help-to], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 1:31], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 13:31], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶find-the-lighttouch-ways], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:52:22])
- **App UI:** On mobile, reserve haptics for confirmations, selection changes (e.g. switching tabs, picking a search result) and errors, light or heavy to match the action. Haptics feel satisfying only in the right places; overused, they annoy. *Note: Use platform haptic presets so system settings are respected, and never make haptics the only signal. iOS Safari has no Vibration API, so web apps can't rely on haptics.* ([raroque/app-design-process 9:29], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 5:20])
- **App UI:** Keep sound off by default; where it's on, as in native mobile apps and hardware-like tools, keep it short, quiet and mutable, and pair it with matching motion and haptics. Tones that signal start, the sweet zone and done can guide users through a task. Results that are seen, heard and felt feel tactile; surgeons knew exactly when to act from the tone. *Note: Editorial: off by default, short, quiet and mutable are not from the sources. Never rely on sound alone; pair it with a visual cue.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶by-combining-dynamic-behaviors], [saffer/microinteractions 12:30])
- **From games:** Reserve shake for rare, meaningful rejections such as a wrong password: a few pixels on one element, never the whole screen. A small shake reads instantly as 'that didn't take', for little effort. Source principle: Add screen shake on impacts, carefully; beyond random shake, kick the camera opposite the firing direction. It shows instantly that the player touched the world. *Note: Disable it under prefers-reduced-motion, and pair it with a text error message.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 2:40], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 12:03], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 16:45])

## Delight and restraint

- Make the product functional, reliable and usable before adding delight; confetti or snarky copy on a flawed product feels gimmicky. Deep delight comes from streamlined workflows and flow. *Note: Concretely, before adding decorative effects, finish the core action's instant pressed state, visible result and clear error state.* ([apple/principles-of-great-design 16:05], [nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶only-when-a-product])
- Keep personality brief and attached to the task ('Howdy partner, ready to schedule your cookout?') and trustworthy; skip humor for serious brands. Personality differentiates, as Slack's did, but overbearing or gimmicky tone feels untrustworthy. ([nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶attempts-at-humor-animation], [slack/easter-eggs ¶while-also-being-trustworthy], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:45:26])
- **App UI:** If the product has clipboard inference or similar implicit input or the user asks for it, use context to offer the likely next step: a tracking code found on the clipboard, higher brightness for a scannable pass. Inferring intent without asking feels magical and pleasantly surprises users. *Note: Make suggestions easy to cancel or change, and ask before reading the clipboard where the platform requires it.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶when-an-interface-makes], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 11:43], [saffer/microinteractions 16:55])
- **App UI:** If the product has reward imagery or the user asks for it, give users concrete, achievable, rewarding goals like inbox zero, and reward completion with imagery chosen for specific emotions such as serenity or awe, using only supplied assets. It widens the emotional range beyond joy into love and surprise. *Note: Use imagery the user or the design system supplies; don't generate or invent it.* ([vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶we-do-this-specifically], [vohra/beyond-gamification 5:00])
- **App UI:** If the product has easter eggs or the user asks for them, reward exploration with pleasant surprises, e.g. hidden behind a slash-command menu, or a 'Surprise me!' setting that plays a brief animation. Fun is a pleasant surprise; pleasure alone is not fun. ([slack/easter-eggs ¶you-found-a-whole], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶a-good-toy-is])
- **App UI:** When users enter painful information, acknowledge it with one empathetic sentence, e.g. 'We're sorry to hear about your loss.'. Acknowledging the moment makes people feel seen. ([walter/designing-for-emotion 0:48:53])
