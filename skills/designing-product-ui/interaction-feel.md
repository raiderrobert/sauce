# Interaction feel

How individual actions feel: the feedback budget, acknowledgement, speed, keyboard feedback, waits and proportional feedback, plus what to add only when the product has it or the user asks.

Practitioner heuristics; the user's scope, accessibility and the product's design system take precedence. **App UI** = product screens only, **Marketing** = landing pages and websites only, **From games** = a product-UI rule translated from a game-design principle (the principle is in the links file), untagged = both. *Note:* = uncited editorial caveat; follow it where it corrects a source number. *(Editorial.)* = a rule with no source. Citations are `author/source locator` (a timestamp, a ¶ passage anchor, or ¶p and a PDF page); their links are in `references/links/interaction-feel.md`.

Contents: Feedback budget · Acknowledge every action · Speed and flow · Keyboard and power-user feedback · Waits, loading and progress · Proportional feedback · Only when asked

## Feedback budget

- **App UI:** Games need a lot of feedback; productivity tools need a little, in the right places, and feedback never compensates for broken function. Size each action's feedback by the rungs below. *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung one: keyboard-triggered and very frequent actions, such as arrowing through a list, selecting a row or opening a command menu, get a state change only (on mobile, plus a light haptic where the haptics rule in Proportional feedback allows), with no motion (see also motion.md § Purpose and restraint). *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung two: routine clicks, such as toggling a setting, checking a task or moving a card, get one small cue per changed element: the control's own state change (the checkbox ticks, the toggle flips), plus one color flash on any other element it changed, such as a row updated from a menu or a count that rose (see Acknowledge every action). Continuous tracking during a drag doesn't count against the cue, which applies to the completed move (see also motion.md § Gestures and input timing). *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung three: completions users care about, such as sending an invoice, publishing a post or finishing an import, get one clear confirmation, e.g. a 'Sent' message or a 'Published' label with a link. *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung four: rare milestones, such as finishing a course or a 100-day streak in a habit app, get layered feedback, only in consumer and mobile apps that are not pro tools (see Proportional feedback). *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Pro tools, meaning tools people use for hours a day on any platform, such as an issue tracker, an email client or a design tool, stop at rung three: no layered feedback, even on mobile. *(Editorial.)*

## Acknowledge every action

- Give every action on a control an immediate visible response, whether by tap, click or shortcut: always a pressed state; a progress label ('Processing your order') only when the wait passes about 1s (see Waits, loading and progress); and a confirmation (a 'Done' label or a 'Copied' chip) only when the result isn't itself visible or the action is a rung-three completion (see Feedback budget). Keyboard actions get this feedback with no motion (see Feedback budget). Without a response the app seems to ignore the user, and a slow action looks like the click didn't register. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-important-for-an], [apple/touch-on-mac 10:56], [jain/beginner-mistakes 5:39], [jain/every-ui-concept 7:31], [jain/macos-apps 6:46], [jain/tiktok-ux 4:06], [saffer/microinteractions 27:45], [vohra/beyond-gamification 8:29])
- **From games:** When an action changes an element other than the control used, flash that element's color; pointer actions may add a slight scale. Feedback on the changed element shows exactly what the input did. *Note: This is rung two's one cue per changed element other than the control, such as a row updated from a menu or a count that rose; the control's own state change, like a checkbox tick, is its cue and needs no flash (see Feedback budget). Keep it to one short flash, well under three flashes per second.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 3:10], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:24], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 11:43])
- **From games:** When a command can't run or a disabled control is used, respond specifically, never silently; at list boundaries, show a non-interrupting inline cue or nothing. Users learn the system's limits from its responses; silence reads as the app being broken. *Note: Commands here are those the app defines: a shortcut, submit or undo. Never stack repeated messages. For a disabled control, say why it's disabled and how to proceed (see also components-and-states.md § Feedback and states). Inside a text field, leave Cmd+Z/Ctrl+Z to the field's own text undo; don't intercept it for app-level undo.* ([jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:15], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 14:27])
- **App UI:** Don't let content change silently after an action the app took on the user's behalf (auto-categorize, AI edit); at minimum show a toast, ideally highlight the changed content itself (see the highlight rule in this section). If users blink they miss it and can't feel the feature working. *Note: Announce the change through a polite live region too; the highlight alone is invisible to screen-reader users.* ([raroque/apps-feel-premium 0:53])
- **App UI:** Communicate invisible rules through feedback as users act, escalating near a limit, e.g. a character counter that turns amber, then red, as text nears the maximum. Rules are invisible; feedback is the only way users understand them. *Note: Editorial: the character-counter example; the source's example is MailChimp's chimp arm stretching as a newsletter grows too long. Show the remaining count too, not color alone.* ([saffer/microinteractions 25:18])
- **From games:** Cue a change outside the user's focus near its trigger or the focused element, and show hidden state like syncing or a failed save. Users, like players, miss changes outside their focus, and invisible state looks like nothing is happening. *Note: For accessibility, announce important status changes in a polite live region. For content the app changed on the user's behalf, see the auto-change rule in this section.* ([valve/half-life-documentary 0:14:14])
- **From games:** Keep evidence of finished work visible (a done count, a completed section, a brief linger before it leaves); this covers completing, not archiving or deleting. Permanence shows users what they accomplished. *Note: In a triage queue, finishing an item opens the next (see Speed and flow).* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 4:21], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 13:07], [valve/half-life-documentary 0:16:36])

## Speed and flow

- Respond the instant an interaction starts: highlight a control on touch down, and show the pressed state or optimistic result before any network call, aiming for under 100ms to first visual response. Remove latencies and timers that crept in during design. People are very sensitive to latency; slower responses invite distraction and break flow, and across hundreds of interactions a day small delays add up. *Note: For optimistic results, see Waits, loading and progress.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-not-just-swipes], [apple/principles-of-great-design 14:08], [eldh/invisible-details ¶some-product-and-engineering], [slack/incremental-boot ¶loading-one-second-faster], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶superhuman-browser-app-is], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶milliseconds-matter-they-may])
- **App UI:** In sequential triage queues (an email inbox, a review queue), auto-advance: when the user finishes an item, e.g. archives an email, open the next one instead of returning to the list. Returning to the list forces a decision every time; hesitation kills flow. *Note: Outside a queue, e.g. after closing a single issue, keep the user where they were. To show one item at a time, see also layout-and-hierarchy.md § Views, grouping and placement.* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 11:38], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶their-next-action-should])
- **App UI:** Pair a double-tap or double-click with a single action only when the single action is harmless and immediate, like select (single selects, double opens); otherwise avoid double actions. A competing single action must wait about half a second to see whether a second tap follows; a selection is correct either way. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶tapping-to-show-the], [apple/touch-on-mac 3:10], [jain/software-must-lie 1:11])

## Keyboard and power-user feedback

- **App UI:** Never drop or misroute fast input: buffer keystrokes so text typed straight after a shortcut, like C then a name, lands in the new field. Ignored inputs feel like a fighting game ignoring half your moves. *Note: For the command menu and single-key shortcuts, see Only when asked; for shortcut hints, see the shortcut-hint rule in this section.* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 8:02])
- **App UI:** On menu selection, briefly blink the chosen item in the accent color, then subtly fade the menu out. Fire the action immediately; the blink never delays it. The blink assures the selection succeeded; the fade is more graceful than vanishing. *Note: Keep it to a single short blink, well under three flashes per second. Fade the menu out in 100ms or less so it stays within no motion (see also motion.md § Purpose and restraint).* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶the-selected-item-briefly])
- **App UI:** Show each action's keyboard shortcut in app and context menus, separated from the label by alignment and type, e.g. right-aligned in secondary text, and show the hint on the action's button too. Teaches and reminds users of shortcuts; the structure shows what is clickable and what is a hint. *Note: A Cmd/Ctrl+K command menu that lists each action with its shortcut teaches shortcuts too (see Only when asked). For teaching shortcuts, see also navigation-and-disclosure.md § Onboarding and engagement; for global shortcuts, see also mobile-and-desktop.md § Native-feeling desktop apps.* ([eldh/invisible-details ¶contextual-menus-are-also], [jain/captivating-sections 4:22], [jain/macos-apps 1:37])

## Waits, loading and progress

- Pick a pending action's feedback by what it does. Reversible, low-risk changes are optimistic: show a delete or send as done at once and run the request in the background, briefly delaying execution where undo matters (e.g. a 5s send delay). Actions that need server validation disable the control at once and, if the request is still pending after about 1s, change its label (e.g. 'Saving…') and optionally add a spinner glyph to it, using the button itself rather than a separate indicator. Content loads show a skeleton where the content will appear, immediately or after a short delay. Long (about 10s or more) or multi-step waits get a progress bar or step labels; a faster-spinning spinner also feels quicker. Optimistic changes avoid awkward pauses while leaving time to undo; a disabled control confirms the click and prevents repeated attempts; an indicator flashed on a fast response reads as lag, while longer waits need visible progress. *Note: Editorial: the thresholds, including a skeleton delay of about 200–300ms rather than 1s; once any indicator is shown, keep it about 300ms so it doesn't flash. If an optimistic request fails, roll back the change and show an error message. Prefer aria-disabled plus aria-busy so focus isn't lost, announce completion, and pair any spinner with text or a polite live-region status.* ([jain/dashboard-ui 7:45], [jain/macos-apps 1:57], [jain/software-must-lie 5:57], [kowalski/great-animations ¶fast-animations-improve-the], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶a-fasterspinning-spinner-makes], [nngroup/disabled-buttons 2:31], [saffer/microinteractions 27:09])
- **App UI:** Make progress indicators carry real information: keep a bar for remaining time, show task counts on the trigger like Chrome downloads, match spinner segments to running processes. Reliable information about the wait reduces its cost, and users don't have to go look. ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p3], [saffer/microinteractions 19:19])
- **App UI:** Fill unavoidable waits that have no layout to skeleton, such as app start-up, with plain loading messages instead of a blank screen, as a stopgap, and still minimize time spent on them; add humor only if the user supplied a playful brand voice. For which indicator each wait gets, see the pending-action rule in this section. It eases idle time and keeps the wait from feeling stalled, but getting users back to their work matters most. *Note: Keep loader animation subtle and skip it under prefers-reduced-motion.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2], [saffer/microinteractions 9:23], [slack/easter-eggs ¶the-custom-loading-messages], [slack/incremental-boot ¶seeing-these-messages-is])
- Show the work itself (each source searched, each criterion matched, results as found) only for operations users value for the effort, such as search aggregation, AI generation or analysis, and only real operations, never fabricated busywork; everywhere else speed wins: never add delay to show effort. Seeing the work raises the perceived value of a result users value. *Note: Throttle a running tally so screen readers aren't flooded.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2])

## Proportional feedback

- **App UI:** Layer feedback on the milestones rung four allows (see Feedback budget), e.g. a button that turns into a check while the background expands and a short message fades in on a spring with no overshoot (see also motion.md § Springs and physics). Matching the emotion of a real accomplishment is what people remember; repeated on routine actions, layering becomes noise. *Note: Follow the Feedback budget. Make the core action work first: confetti or flourishes on a product that isn't yet functional, reliable and usable feel gimmicky. Respect prefers-reduced-motion, keep layered effects short so they never delay the next action, and add sound only as in Only when asked.* ([apple/principles-of-great-design 16:05], [nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶subtle-animations-help-to], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 1:31], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 13:31], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶find-the-lighttouch-ways], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:52:22])
- **App UI:** On mobile, reserve haptics for confirmations, selection changes (e.g. switching tabs, picking a search result) and errors, light or heavy to match the action. Haptics feel satisfying only in the right places; overused, they annoy. *Note: Use platform haptic presets so system settings are respected, and never make haptics the only signal. iOS Safari has no Vibration API, so web apps can't rely on haptics. For gesture feedback, see also mobile-and-desktop.md § Gestures and feedback.* ([raroque/app-design-process 9:29], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 5:20])
- **From games:** Reserve shake for rare, meaningful rejections such as a wrong password submitted by pointer or touch; after a keyboard submit, show the color change and text error without the shake (for its size and reduced-motion handling, see also motion.md § Purpose and restraint). A small shake reads instantly as 'that didn't take', for little effort. *Note: Pair it with a text error message.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 2:40], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 12:03], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 16:45])

## Only when asked

- **App UI:** If the product has a command menu or the user asks for it, open it with Cmd/Ctrl+K, let users type to find any action and see its shortcut, and make every action reachable from it; all controls stay keyboard-operable either way. Users knew what they wanted but forgot how; the palette teaches the shortcut for next time. *Note: Prevent the browser default for Cmd/Ctrl+K unless a text editor that owns Cmd/Ctrl+K has focus (rich-text editors use Cmd+K to insert a link). For other shortcut rules, see Keyboard and power-user feedback.* ([eldh/invisible-details ¶you-can-take-almost], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶hundred-milliseconds-response-times], [vohra/beyond-gamification 13:10])
- **App UI:** If the product already has single-key shortcuts or the user asks for them, reserve them for the most frequent actions, such as C to compose. A frequent action then takes one keystroke. *Note: Don't let single-key shortcuts fire while typing in a field, and let users turn off or remap them (WCAG 2.1.4).* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 8:02])
- **App UI:** If the product has clipboard inference or similar implicit input or the user asks for it, use context to offer the likely next step: a tracking code found on the clipboard, higher brightness for a scannable pass. Inferring intent without asking feels magical and pleasantly surprises users. *Note: Make suggestions easy to cancel or change, and ask before reading the clipboard where the platform requires it.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶when-an-interface-makes], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 11:43], [saffer/microinteractions 16:55])
- **App UI:** Add sound only if the product already has sound or the user asks for it: keep it short, quiet and mutable, and pair it with matching motion, plus haptics only where the haptics rule in Proportional feedback allows them. Tones that signal start, the sweet zone and done can guide users through a task. Results that are seen, heard and felt feel tactile; surgeons knew exactly when to act from the tone. *Note: Editorial: the has-sound-or-asked gate and short, quiet and mutable are not from the sources. Never rely on sound alone; pair it with a visual cue.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶by-combining-dynamic-behaviors], [saffer/microinteractions 12:30])
- If the user supplied a playful brand voice, add personality to copy, kept brief, attached to the task ('Howdy partner, ready to schedule your cookout?') and trustworthy; otherwise keep product UI copy plain and task-focused (for landing-page copy, see also landing-pages.md § Copy). Personality differentiates, as Slack's did, but overbearing or gimmicky tone feels untrustworthy. *Note: Whatever the voice, when users enter painful information, acknowledge it with one plain empathetic sentence ('We're sorry to hear about your loss').* ([nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶attempts-at-humor-animation], [slack/easter-eggs ¶while-also-being-trustworthy], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:45:26])
- **App UI:** If the product has reward imagery or the user asks for it, give users concrete, achievable, rewarding goals like inbox zero, and reward completion with imagery chosen for specific emotions such as serenity or awe, using only supplied assets. It widens the emotional range beyond joy into love and surprise. *Note: In a pro tool, a single image counts as the one clear confirmation (see Feedback budget). This rewards a real goal; it is not points or badges (see also navigation-and-disclosure.md § Onboarding and engagement).* ([vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶we-do-this-specifically], [vohra/beyond-gamification 5:00])
- **App UI:** If the product has easter eggs or the user asks for them, reward exploration with pleasant surprises, e.g. hidden behind a slash-command menu, or a 'Surprise me!' setting that plays a brief animation. Fun is a pleasant surprise; pleasure alone is not fun. ([slack/easter-eggs ¶you-found-a-whole], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶a-good-toy-is])
