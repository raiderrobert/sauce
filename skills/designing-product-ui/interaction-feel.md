# Interaction feel

How individual actions feel: the feedback budget, acknowledgement, speed, keyboard feedback, waits, proportional feedback and delight, and when to hold back.

Practitioner heuristics; the user's scope, accessibility and the product's design system take precedence. **App UI** = product screens only, **Marketing** = landing pages and websites only, **From games** = a product-UI rule translated from a game-design principle (the principle is in the links file), untagged = both. *Note:* = uncited editorial caveat; follow it where it corrects a source number. Citations are `author/source locator` (a timestamp, a ¶ passage anchor, or ¶p and a PDF page); their links are in `references/links/interaction-feel.md`.

## Feedback budget

- **App UI:** Rung one: keyboard and very frequent actions get a state change only, with no motion: no movement or scale; a color change, one blink, or an opacity fade of 100ms or less is allowed. For which triggers and surfaces count, see also motion.md. *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung two: routine clicks get one small cue on the affected element (see the highlight rule in Acknowledge every action). *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung three: completions users care about get one clear confirmation. *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Rung four: rare milestones get layered feedback, only in consumer and mobile apps that are not pro tools (see the layered-feedback rule in Proportional feedback). *(Editorial.)*
- **App UI:** Pro tools, meaning tools people use for hours a day on any platform, stop at rung three: no layered feedback, even on mobile. *(Editorial.)*

## Acknowledge every action

- Give every action on a control an immediate visible response, whether by tap, click or shortcut: a pressed state, a progress label ('Processing your order') while it runs (see also the wait rules in Waits, loading and progress), and a confirmation when done (a 'Done' label or a 'Copied' chip). Keyboard actions get this feedback with no motion (see also motion.md). Without a response the app seems to ignore the user, and a slow action looks like the click didn't register. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-important-for-an], [apple/touch-on-mac 10:56], [jain/beginner-mistakes 5:39], [jain/every-ui-concept 7:31], [jain/macos-apps 6:46], [jain/tiktok-ux 4:06], [saffer/microinteractions 27:45], [vohra/beyond-gamification 8:29])
- **From games:** Show an action's result on the affected element itself: a color flash; pointer actions may add a slight scale. Feedback on the changed element shows exactly what the input did. *Note: This is the one small cue routine clicks get (see Feedback budget). Keep it to one short flash, well under three flashes per second.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 3:10], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:24], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 11:43])
- **From games:** When a command the app defines (a shortcut, submit, undo) can't run, or a disabled control is used, give a specific, friendly response, never silence. Users learn the system's limits from its responses; silence reads as the app being broken. *Note: At list boundaries, such as arrowing past the last row, stay silent or show a non-interrupting inline cue; never stack repeated messages. For a disabled control, say why it's disabled and how to proceed (see also the disabled-button rules in components-and-states.md). Inside a text field, leave Cmd+Z/Ctrl+Z to the field's own text undo; don't intercept it for app-level undo.* ([jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 1:15], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 14:27])
- **App UI:** Don't let content change silently after an action the app took on the user's behalf (auto-categorize, AI edit); at minimum show a toast, ideally highlight the changed content itself (see the highlight rule in this section). If users blink they miss it and can't feel the feature working. *Note: Announce the change through a polite live region too; the highlight alone is invisible to screen-reader users.* ([raroque/apps-feel-premium 0:53])
- **App UI:** Communicate invisible rules through feedback as users act, escalating near a limit: MailChimp's chimp arm stretches, then pops off, as a newsletter grows too long. Rules are invisible; feedback is the only way users understand them. ([saffer/microinteractions 25:18])
- **From games:** When a change happens away from where the user is working, put a cue near the control that triggered it or the focused element. Users, like players, miss changes outside their focus. *Note: For accessibility, announce important status changes in a polite live region.* ([valve/half-life-documentary 0:14:14])
- **From games:** Show hidden state such as syncing or a failed save. Invisible state looks like nothing is happening. *Note: For accessibility, announce important status changes in a polite live region.* ([valve/half-life-documentary 0:39:48])
- **From games:** Don't remove a completed item the moment it's checked; show it checked in place and leave later removal to the product's convention. Permanence shows users what they accomplished. *Note: In a triage queue, finishing an item opens the next (see the auto-advance rule in Speed and flow).* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 4:21], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 13:07], [valve/half-life-documentary 0:16:36])

## Speed and flow

- Respond the instant an interaction starts: highlight a control on touch down, and show the pressed state or optimistic result before any network call, aiming for under 100ms to first visual response. Remove latencies and timers that crept in during design. People are very sensitive to latency; slower responses invite distraction and break flow, and across hundreds of interactions a day small delays add up. *Note: For an optimistic result, show the change as done and roll it back with an error if the request fails (see also the optimistic-update rule in components-and-states.md).* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-not-just-swipes], [apple/principles-of-great-design 14:08], [eldh/invisible-details ¶some-product-and-engineering], [slack/incremental-boot ¶loading-one-second-faster], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶superhuman-browser-app-is], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶milliseconds-matter-they-may])
- **App UI:** Show search results as soon as typing starts: query local cache and server at once, merge live, and stage inserts to avoid pop-in. A separate 'search the server' step pushes users to switch tasks while waiting. *Note: Announce result counts politely to screen readers and don't move focus as results merge.* ([apple/intuitive-search 11:50], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶it-is-also-simultaneously])
- **App UI:** In sequential triage queues (an email inbox, a review queue), auto-advance: when the user finishes an item, e.g. archives an email, open the next one instead of returning to the list. Returning to the list forces a decision every time; hesitation kills flow. *Note: Outside a queue, e.g. after closing a single issue, keep the user where they were.* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 11:38], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶their-next-action-should])
- **App UI:** If the product is built to process a queue to zero, propose showing one item at a time without the list beside it. Seeing what's next makes users distractible. *Note: Where users compare or navigate entries, keep a split view (see also the search-placement rule in layout-and-hierarchy.md).* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 13:25], [vohra/designing-for-flow ¶in-superhuman-you-cant])
- **App UI:** Pair a double-tap or double-click with a single action only when the single action is harmless and immediate, like select (single selects, double opens); otherwise avoid double actions. A competing single action must wait about half a second to see whether a second tap follows; a selection is correct either way. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶tapping-to-show-the], [apple/touch-on-mac 3:10], [jain/software-must-lie 1:11])

## Keyboard and power-user feedback

- **App UI:** Give keyboard actions feedback but no motion, even when they open a modal (see also motion.md for which triggers count). Keyboard actions may repeat hundreds of times a day, and motion makes them feel slow, delayed and disconnected from the user's action. *Note: A keyboard action still needs a visible result (see Acknowledge every action).* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶sometimes-we-can-get], [kowalski/great-animations ¶a-good-tip-here], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶an-animation-would-make])
- **App UI:** Give overlays opened hundreds of times a day, such as command menus and app switchers, no motion whatever the trigger (see also motion.md). At that frequency even snappy motion feels slow; without it, moving between apps feels snappy. *Note: Context menus also get no motion, while a pointer-opened dropdown or popover may grow from its trigger in about 120–180ms (see also the menu-motion rule in motion.md).* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶if-we-for-a], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶if-it-animated-every])
- **App UI:** If the product has a command menu or the user asks for it, open it with Cmd/Ctrl+K, let users type to find any action and see its shortcut, and make every action reachable from it; all controls stay keyboard-operable either way. Users knew what they wanted but forgot how; the palette teaches the shortcut for next time. *Note: Cmd+K inserts a link in rich-text editors, so leave it to the editor while one has focus; Ctrl+K is a browser search shortcut in some browsers, so prevent its default only while the app has focus.* ([eldh/invisible-details ¶you-can-take-almost], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶hundred-milliseconds-response-times], [vohra/beyond-gamification 13:10])
- **App UI:** If the product already has single-key shortcuts or the user asks for them, reserve them for the most frequent actions, such as C to compose. A frequent action then takes one keystroke. *Note: Don't let single-key shortcuts fire while typing in a field, and let users turn off or remap them (WCAG 2.1.4).* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 8:02])
- **App UI:** Never drop or misroute fast input: buffer keystrokes so text typed straight after a shortcut, like C then a name, lands in the new field. Ignored inputs feel like a fighting game ignoring half your moves. ([vohra/beyond-gamification 8:02])
- **App UI:** On menu selection, briefly blink the chosen item in the accent color, then subtly fade the menu out. Fire the action immediately; the blink never delays it. The blink assures the selection succeeded; the fade is more graceful than vanishing. *Note: Keep it to a single short blink, well under three flashes per second. Fade the menu out in 100ms or less so it stays within no motion (see also motion.md).* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶the-selected-item-briefly])
- **App UI:** Make inputs forgiving: accept loose text like '3d' or '8am in Tokyo' and do your best to interpret it. Forgiving inputs invite playful exploration and pleasant surprise. *Note: Echo the interpreted value (e.g. the resolved date) so users can confirm it.* ([vohra/beyond-gamification 9:07])

## Waits, loading and progress

- Pick wait feedback by duration: instant (under about 100ms) gets no indicator; short (about 100ms to 1s) gets the pressed or optimistic state, then the result, with no spinner; longer (over about 1s) gets a spinner or skeleton where the content will appear; long (about 10s or more) or multi-step waits get a progress bar or step labels. A faster-spinning spinner also feels quicker. An indicator flashed on a fast response reads as lag, while longer waits need visible progress. *Note: Editorial: the thresholds. Duration is usually unknown: show the pressed state at once, show the spinner or skeleton only if still pending after about 1s, and once shown keep it about 300ms so it doesn't flash. For a submit button, see the next rule. Pair any spinner with text or a polite live-region status.* ([kowalski/great-animations ¶fast-animations-improve-the], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶a-fasterspinning-spinner-makes])
- Use what's already on screen for feedback: on submit, disable the button and change its label at once (e.g. 'Saving…'), and add a spinner glyph to it only if the request is still pending after about 1s. It confirms the click without adding a progress bar and prevents repeated attempts. *Note: Prefer aria-disabled plus aria-busy so focus isn't lost, and announce completion.* ([nngroup/disabled-buttons 2:31], [saffer/microinteractions 27:09])
- **App UI:** Make progress indicators carry real information: keep a bar for remaining time, show task counts on the trigger like Chrome downloads, match spinner segments to running processes. Reliable information about the wait reduces its cost, and users don't have to go look. ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p3], [saffer/microinteractions 19:19])
- **App UI:** Fill unavoidable waits with plain loading messages instead of a blank screen, as a stopgap, and still minimize time spent on them; add humor only if the user supplied a playful brand voice. It eases idle time and keeps the wait from feeling stalled, but getting users back to their work matters most. *Note: Keep loader animation subtle and skip it under prefers-reduced-motion.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2], [saffer/microinteractions 9:23], [slack/easter-eggs ¶the-custom-loading-messages], [slack/incremental-boot ¶seeing-these-messages-is])
- Show the work itself (each source searched, each criterion matched, results as found) only for operations users value for the effort, such as search aggregation, AI generation or analysis; everywhere else speed wins: never add delay to show effort. Seeing the work raises the perceived value of a result users value. *Note: Throttle a running tally so screen readers aren't flooded.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p2])
- **App UI:** Keep show-the-work waits short and cut long ones: the benefit of showing work declined after ~30 s for flight search and ~15 s for dating search. Value drops as waits lengthen, and delays that look avoidable cause uncertainty and anger. *Note: Calibrate to what users expect from comparable services.* ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p14])
- **App UI:** Prefer showing real operations over fabricated busywork, and don't spotlight effort when the result is likely poor. Visible effort followed by a bad result scored worst of all, as users blame the service; faking labor nears an ethical line and backfires if users notice. ([buell-norton/labor-illusion ¶p12])

## Proportional feedback

- **App UI:** Layer feedback only on rare milestones, and only in consumer and mobile apps that are not pro tools, e.g. a button that turns into a check while the background expands and a short message springs in; completions users care about get one clear confirmation, and routine actions keep one small cue. Matching the emotion of a real accomplishment is what people remember; repeated on routine actions, layering becomes noise. *Note: Follow the Feedback budget: pro tools stop at one clear confirmation. Respect prefers-reduced-motion, keep layered effects short so they never delay the next action, add sound only under the sound rule in this section, and skip tacked-on confetti.* ([nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶subtle-animations-help-to], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 1:31], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 13:31], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶find-the-lighttouch-ways], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:52:22])
- **App UI:** On mobile, reserve haptics for confirmations, selection changes (e.g. switching tabs, picking a search result) and errors, light or heavy to match the action. Haptics feel satisfying only in the right places; overused, they annoy. *Note: Use platform haptic presets so system settings are respected, and never make haptics the only signal. iOS Safari has no Vibration API, so web apps can't rely on haptics.* ([raroque/app-design-process 9:29], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 5:20])
- **App UI:** Add sound only if the product already has sound: keep it short, quiet and mutable, and pair it with matching motion and haptics. Tones that signal start, the sweet zone and done can guide users through a task. Results that are seen, heard and felt feel tactile; surgeons knew exactly when to act from the tone. *Note: Editorial: the already-has-sound gate and short, quiet and mutable are not from the sources. Never rely on sound alone; pair it with a visual cue.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶by-combining-dynamic-behaviors], [saffer/microinteractions 12:30])
- **From games:** Reserve shake for rare, meaningful rejections such as a wrong password: a few pixels on one element, never the whole screen. A small shake reads instantly as 'that didn't take', for little effort. *Note: Disable it under prefers-reduced-motion, and pair it with a text error message.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 2:40], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 12:03], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 16:45])

## Delight and restraint

- Make the product functional, reliable and usable before adding delight; confetti or snarky copy on a flawed product feels gimmicky. Deep delight comes from streamlined workflows and flow. *Note: Concretely, before adding decorative effects, finish the core action's instant pressed state, visible result and clear error state.* ([apple/principles-of-great-design 16:05], [nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶only-when-a-product])
- Default to plain, task-focused copy; only if the user supplied a playful brand voice, add personality, kept brief, attached to the task ('Howdy partner, ready to schedule your cookout?') and trustworthy. Personality differentiates, as Slack's did, but overbearing or gimmicky tone feels untrustworthy. ([nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶attempts-at-humor-animation], [slack/easter-eggs ¶while-also-being-trustworthy], [walter/designing-for-emotion 0:45:26])
- **App UI:** If the product has clipboard inference or similar implicit input or the user asks for it, use context to offer the likely next step: a tracking code found on the clipboard, higher brightness for a scannable pass. Inferring intent without asking feels magical and pleasantly surprises users. *Note: Make suggestions easy to cancel or change, and ask before reading the clipboard where the platform requires it.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶when-an-interface-makes], [raroque/apps-feel-premium 11:43], [saffer/microinteractions 16:55])
- **App UI:** If the product has reward imagery or the user asks for it, give users concrete, achievable, rewarding goals like inbox zero, and reward completion with imagery chosen for specific emotions such as serenity or awe, using only supplied assets. It widens the emotional range beyond joy into love and surprise. *Note: Use imagery the user or the design system supplies; don't generate or invent it. This rewards a real goal; it is not points or badges (see also the no-bolted-on-points rule in navigation-and-disclosure.md).* ([vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶we-do-this-specifically], [vohra/beyond-gamification 5:00])
- **App UI:** If the product has easter eggs or the user asks for them, reward exploration with pleasant surprises, e.g. hidden behind a slash-command menu, or a 'Surprise me!' setting that plays a brief animation. Fun is a pleasant surprise; pleasure alone is not fun. ([slack/easter-eggs ¶you-found-a-whole], [vohra/acquired-feel-like-a-game ¶a-good-toy-is])
- **App UI:** When users enter painful information, acknowledge it with one empathetic sentence, e.g. 'We're sorry to hear about your loss.'. Acknowledging the moment makes people feel seen. ([walter/designing-for-emotion 0:48:53])
