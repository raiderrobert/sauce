# Motion

Purpose, easing and timing, hover and press feedback, transitions, gestures, loaders and entrances, and scroll and ambient motion.

Practitioner heuristics; the user's scope, accessibility and the product's design system take precedence. **App UI** = product screens only, **Marketing** = landing pages and websites only, **From games** = a product-UI rule translated from a game-design principle (the principle is in the links file), untagged = both. *Note:* = uncited editorial caveat; follow it where it corrects a source number. *(Editorial.)* = a rule with no source. Citations are `author/source locator` (a timestamp, a ¶ passage anchor, or ¶p and a PDF page); their links are in `references/links/motion.md`.

## Purpose and restraint

- Animate only to add clarity, feedback, focus, object permanence or delight, e.g. to direct the eye to the product or key text; not motion for its own sake. Purposeless animation distracts instead of supporting clarity, and animations everywhere lose their impact. *Note: Honor prefers-reduced-motion: reduce or remove non-essential animation, replacing movement with an opacity fade.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶object-permanence-creating-a], [jain/boring-design-hacks 5:08], [jain/captivating-sections 2:22], [jain/genius-designer 3:32], [jain/vibe-coding-cant-fix 7:37], [kowalski/great-animations ¶its-easy-to-start], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶before-you-start-animating])
- Decide whether to animate by trigger first: keyboard-triggered changes get no motion, even a modal. A keyboard trigger is any keydown-initiated open or change, including Enter or Space on a focused control; press-state color still applies, press scale does not. Keyboard actions repeat often, and motion makes them feel slow. *Note: No motion means no movement or scale; a color change, one blink, or an opacity fade of 100ms or less is allowed. To tell a keyboard open from a pointer open, record the last input type on keydown and pointerdown (e.g. a `data-input` attribute on the root) and gate transitions on it.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶sometimes-we-can-get], [kowalski/great-animations ¶a-good-tip-here], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶an-animation-would-make])
- Then decide by how often a surface is seen. No motion, whatever the trigger: command menu, context menu, app switcher, keyboard navigation and selection, row selection, repeated tooltips, typing, and in pro tools on desktop, tab and page switches (see also interaction-feel.md § Feedback budget). Motion OK, rather than appearing instantly, when pointer or touch opens them: modals, drawers, sheets, toasts, mobile push navigation, completions, first-run moments. Judge anything else by its thousandth use. A pleasant surprise once becomes irritating and slow when seen daily; at hundreds of opens a day, even snappy motion feels slow. On occasional surfaces, instant changes feel artificial. *Note: No motion is as defined in the trigger rule in this section. Editorial: the placement of tab and page switches.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶not-get-in-the], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶it-doesnt-feel-like], [kowalski/great-animations ¶before-you-add-an], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶how-often-users-will], [raroque/apps-feel-10x-better 0:52], [saffer/microinteractions 31:21])
- Keep animations subtle and simple rather than big or flashy, and execute them cleanly at full quality. Check: if the movement draws the eye away from the content it carries, shorten the distance or scale. Flashy animations slow the site and overwhelm people; blurry assets look sloppy. *Note: Editorial examples for product UI: a fade, a short slide (about 8-24px) or a slight scale (about 0.95-1.05) rather than large sweeps, spins and bounces. On marketing pages, see the expressive-motion rule in this section.* ([jain/captivating-sections 4:06], [jain/modern-layouts 4:24], [jain/think-like-product-designer 0:47], [jain/trendy-web-animations 0:06], [nngroup/theory-of-user-delight ¶combine-this-with-flashy])
- **Marketing:** Expressive motion belongs on marketing pages: there, motion can be used even where static would work, built from a few repeated themes (e.g. consistent section transitions) that become the brand's animation identity. Repeated animations, not one-offs, form a motion identity. ([jain/boring-design-hacks 2:50], [jain/vibe-coding-cant-fix 7:27])
- **From games:** Keep shakes and large movements small and remove them under prefers-reduced-motion; don't raise their intensity as you grow used to them. Designers become desensitized to their own effects while first-time users feel the full intensity. *Note: Editorial caps for product UI: shake a few pixels on one element; scale within about 0.95–1.05 (see the subtlety rule in this section); on marketing pages, see the expressive-motion rule. Rely on prefers-reduced-motion rather than adding an in-app toggle nobody asked for.* ([nijman/art-of-screenshake 32:19])
- **App UI:** Keep dashboard animation tame; spend creativity on chart hovers such as value bubbles or dimming the other bars. ([jain/dashboard-ui 7:23])

## Easing and timing

- Choose the easing curve for the tone you want: snappy and performant, fun and springy, or slow and smooth (e.g. a slightly slower ease for an elegant toast). In product UI, the choice stays within the fast ease-out rule below and the no-overshoot spring default (see Springs and physics). The easing defines how an animation feels. *Note: Editorial mapping: snappy = a short ease-out, springy = a spring with slight overshoot (marketing only; in product UI see Springs and physics), smooth = a longer ease-in-out.* ([jain/boring-design-hacks 3:40], [jain/mobile-swipe-interactions 0:44], [kowalski/great-animations ¶uses-ease-as-the])
- **App UI:** In product UI, keep animations fast: under about 300ms with ease-out, e.g. a ~180ms dropdown rather than 400ms; for menus by trigger, see the menu-motion rule in Transitions and overlays. Ease-out starts fast, so the interface feels responsive and like it is listening. *Note: Marketing sites can run slower (see the Marketing easing rules in this section).* ([kowalski/great-animations ¶your-animations-should-also], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶unless-you-are-working])
- **From games:** Ease pointer-triggered or automatic view moves (jumping to an anchor, following an object on a canvas) into place instead of hard-snapping; keep keyboard list navigation and other keyboard-triggered moves instant (see the trigger rule in Purpose and restraint). Motion that is fast at first and smooths in feels better, even when nobody notices it. *Note: In CSS, an ease-out curve gives the same deceleration; under prefers-reduced-motion, jump instead.* ([jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 3:59], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 14:53])
- **Marketing:** On marketing pages and load sequences, move large elements (reveals, expansions, wipes) with an exponential ease-in-out; the sources use about 1.3s for a full-screen slide-up and about 400ms for an expanding navbar. Large movements need a strong curve. *Note: In CSS, an expo ease-in-out is about cubic-bezier(0.87, 0, 0.13, 1).* ([jain/load-animations-code 5:32], [jain/load-animations-figma 4:55])
- **Marketing:** On marketing pages and load sequences, fade and transition small elements with a simple ease-in-out of about 200-300ms; keep the exponential curve for large movements. *Note: In app UI, use the short ease-out rule in this section instead.* ([jain/load-animations-figma 4:55])
- **Marketing:** Use a spring rather than default easing for hover pop-ups, e.g. the source's 500ms spring with stiffness 636 and damping 24. The spring is what makes the pop-up feel alive. *Note: In the Motion library, { type: 'spring', stiffness: 636, damping: 24 }; Figma and code springs may not match exactly.* ([jain/micro-animations 3:06])

## Springs and physics

- Use springs for natural motion, e.g. pulling overscrolled content back within bounds; tune them by damping (overshoot) and response (speed) rather than duration. Springs are seamless, can be retargeted at any time, and raw physics properties are unwieldy. *Note: Web libraries such as Motion expose stiffness, damping and mass instead; SwiftUI's spring(response:dampingFraction:) matches the two-parameter model.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶the-content-uses-elasticity], [kowalski/great-animations ¶i-highly-suggest-playing])
- Default springs to no overshoot (100% damping), as for tap-triggered presentations; add a little bounce (about 80% damping) only when a gesture carried momentum, like swipe-to-dismiss, or to hint at a hidden gesture. Move remaining cards forward as the top card is swiped away. A tap has no momentum; rewarding a swipe's momentum feels satisfying and rigid swiping feels unnatural, while a bounce on tap hints something lies underneath. *Note: The bouncier hover pop-up spring in Easing and timing is a marketing flourish.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶so-we-recommend-starting], [jain/micro-animations 6:48], [jain/mobile-swipe-interactions 0:33], [saffer/microinteractions 15:10])
- Tune motion to conceptual weight: give lighter objects, like a photo or a playing card, less mass and less bounce. Motion should match how heavy the object feels conceptually. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶theres-less-mass-on], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶exhibits-less-bounce-since])
- Treat motion as one family: an object moves the same way whatever drives it, scroll friction stays consistent, and all screens share one motion language (same curves, springs and directions). People learn by repetition, so one learned behavior transfers. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-important-that-you])
- Project gesture momentum onto scale and rotation as well as position, e.g. when a pinch or rotate gesture is released. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶you-can-also-use])
- For fast movement, limit change between frames and encode motion with blur or stretching along velocity. Too much change between frames strobes even at a steady frame rate. *Note: In product UI, keep any stretch subtle, and drop it under prefers-reduced-motion. Animate transform and opacity rather than padding or margin to hold 60fps, which lets objects move faster without strobing; when the main thread is busy, use CSS or WAAPI rather than requestAnimationFrame.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶its-not-just-about], [kowalski/great-animations ¶you-should-try-to])
- Make motion seamless and familiar: no sudden changes in velocity or direction, an imperceptible stop, and real-world behaviors like coasting, friction and elasticity. Believable motion matters most; it need not be a literal simulation. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶the-line-is-completely])

## Hover and press feedback

- **Marketing:** Add small, simple hover responses to interactive elements, including product visuals and bento cards; don't go overboard. They keep simple pages from feeling bland. ([jain/boring-design-hacks 5:21], [jain/landing-page-levels 6:35], [jain/modern-layouts 5:02])
- **Marketing:** On marketing pages, give buttons a hover animation beyond a color shift, e.g. slide the label up to reveal a duplicate while a background fill expands. Looks polished without juggling many hover colors. ([jain/genius-designer 4:20], [jain/micro-animations 0:30], [jain/trendy-web-animations 2:46])
- **Marketing:** On card hover, zoom (and optionally blur) the image slightly; for fading in an arrow as the link cue on repeating cards, see also components-and-states.md § Cards and carousels. Adds a premium feel. ([jain/google-ai-redesign 4:41], [jain/tesla-wannabe-redesign 4:12])
- Shrink buttons slightly while pressed and return without overshoot on release. Confirms the click and helps the interface feel alive and responsive. *Note: A keyboard press (Enter or Space on a focused button) keeps the press color but not the scale (see the trigger rule in Purpose and restraint).* ([jain/micro-animations 0:34], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶another-purposeful-animation-is])
- **Marketing:** Use mouse-follow effects sparingly (the best use is pulling a CTA toward the cursor), and give every pointer-driven effect a touch fallback. Mouse-follow can be very annoying, and it doesn't exist on touch devices. ([jain/trendy-web-animations 4:54])

## Transitions and overlays

- Where the frequency rule in Purpose and restraint allows motion, animate a view that expands from a content item (card to detail, thumbnail to viewer) out of that item, arranged the same way so it feels like an expansion, and exit the same way; use another direction only when sending the item somewhere. Sheets, drawers and push navigation follow the direction rule below instead. Skip origin motion when only one element could have launched the view. It mimics object permanence and shows where content came from; with a single possible origin, there's nothing to clarify. *Note: Under prefers-reduced-motion, use a short fade or an instant swap. For menus, popovers and tooltips, see the menu-motion rule in this section.* ([apple/design-foundations 9:27], [apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶it-should-have-a], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶through-motion-this-helps], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶because-it-comes-from], [ording/rubber-band ¶when-he-selected-a])
- Choose menu motion by menu type and trigger. Context (right-click) menus, command menus and app switchers get no motion whatever the trigger, and keyboard-opened menus get none either (see the trigger and frequency rules in Purpose and restraint). This covers the menu surface: on a touch long-press, the pressed item's lift and the screen blur stay (see also mobile-and-desktop.md § Gestures and feedback). A pointer-opened dropdown or popover grows from its trigger edge: scale about 0.95→1 plus a fade, about 120–180ms, origin at the trigger. Hover pop-ups and a first tooltip slide a few pixels toward their final position while fading in. Menus opened many times a day feel slow with any motion; growing from the trigger shows where a pointer-opened menu came from, and a short slide is nicer than a plain fade. *Note: Editorial: the 0.95 scale, the 120ms lower bound and the trigger origin are not from the sources; the ~180ms dropdown and the hover slide are. A context menu may still blink the chosen item and fade out in 100ms or less on close (see also interaction-feel.md § Keyboard and power-user feedback). Once one tooltip is open, neighbouring tooltips open instantly without motion (see also components-and-states.md § Buttons, chips and icons). Under prefers-reduced-motion, fade only.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶it-feels-right-to], [jain/animated-sidebar 3:08], [jain/micro-animations 2:59], [kowalski/great-animations ¶a-good-tip-here], [kowalski/you-dont-need-animations ¶a-180ms-dropdown-animation])
- Make every transition interruptible: users can reverse a swipe mid-navigation or touch content while it animates in, never waiting for motion to finish. Thought and gesture then run in parallel; waiting for an animation feels unresponsive. *Note: In CSS, transitions retarget mid-flight while keyframe animations restart, so prefer transitions for interruptible state changes.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶we-want-to-allow], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶great-interactions-are-modeled], [kowalski/great-animations ¶it-allows-the-user])
- **App UI:** Where the frequency rule in Purpose and restraint allows motion, use motion direction to situate users: temporary actions slide up from the bottom, progress slides in from the side, and page transitions follow the swipe direction. Views that expand from a content item follow the origin rule above. Direction tells users where they are in the flow. ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶if-the-app-slides], [jain/captivating-sections 4:58], [jain/mobile-swipe-interactions 3:19])
- **App UI:** As a bottom sheet rises, scale the background down slightly and move it down; reverse on dismiss. Reinforces that the sheet sits above the page. ([jain/mobile-app-ui 5:26], [jain/mobile-swipe-interactions 5:03])
- **App UI:** Use a layer's entry direction to signal how it is dismissed: a layer sliding in from the right is dismissed by swiping back. Motion communicates the interface's stacked-layer structure. *Note: Also provide a visible back or close control for users who don't swipe.* ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶a-layer-slides-over])
- When items enter or exit a list, pair an opacity change with the height animation. ([kowalski/great-animations ¶the-opacity-change-in])
- **From games:** When a pointer or touch action removes items, animate them out in its direction and visually distinguish them from items that remain. Direction ties the removal to the action, and contrast keeps leaving items from passing as live ones. *Note: Items removed by a keyboard action, such as archiving by key, get no motion (see the trigger rule in Purpose and restraint). Under prefers-reduced-motion, fade removed items out instead.* ([jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 10:39])
- **App UI:** For swipe-back transitions, offset the background screen about 35% left and slide it right as the top screen leaves. Makes the transition feel continuous. ([jain/mobile-app-ui 5:17])
- **Marketing:** On marketing pages and hero moments, transition by zooming a shared image into the next page's hero instead of sliding a new page in; app navigation follows the swipe direction instead. Creates continuity between screens. ([jain/mobile-swipe-interactions 4:03])
- **Marketing:** Add a brief blur to tab and section content transitions. A small change that makes switches feel much smoother. *Note: In CSS, filter: blur().* ([jain/landing-page-levels 8:36])

## Gestures and input timing

- **App UI:** Give continuous feedback during a gesture: track the input one-to-one and grow intermediate states toward the final state, not a threshold-only 0→1 jump. Threshold-only animations give no affordance; people predict trajectories and notice any lost tracking. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶it-grows-from-the], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶feeling-the-scale-delta])
- **App UI:** Make touch feel attached, in this sequence: on a tap, shrink the control slightly while pressed (see also the press rule in Hover and press feedback) and cancel the tap if dragged outside; once a tap-and-hold picks an item up for dragging or its context menu, scale it up while it stays attached to the finger; then drag it with the touch offset preserved (for tilt, see the drag rule below). Touch and content should move together, and users can change their mind mid-press. *Note: Re-highlight the control if the pointer moves back inside before release.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶we-should-respect-the], [apple/touch-on-mac 1:26])
- **From games:** When dragging an item, the position tracks the pointer 1:1; add a slight tilt in the direction of travel. Small physical reactions make on-screen objects feel real and responsive. *Note: Honor prefers-reduced-motion.* ([gmtk/game-feel-and-juice 3:24], [jonasson-purho/juice-it-or-lose-it 5:58], [nijman/art-of-screenshake 19:54], [sakamoto/hearthstone-immersive-ui 10:33])
- **App UI:** When dragging to reorder, show the predicted drop location and auto-scroll faster the closer the pointer is to the edge. A gradient of speed beats a binary on/off. ([jain/software-must-lie 4:14])
- **App UI:** On release, carry the gesture's momentum and angle into the content, and pick snap targets from the momentum-projected position using the platform's scroll deceleration rate. A light flick then lands where expected, reusing people's intuition for how far scrolled content travels. *Note: On the web, keep native scrolling and CSS scroll-snap; apply this only to custom drag surfaces.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶you-want-to-take], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶notice-how-the-gesture])
- **App UI:** Rubber-band at scroll limits: past the edge, move content slower than the finger, then snap back on release; don't hard-stop or pad static space. A hard stop is indistinguishable from a frozen device. *Note: On the web, keep native scrolling and CSS scroll-snap; apply this only to custom drag surfaces.* ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶you-actually-wouldnt-know], [ording/rubber-band ¶when-he-got-to])
- **App UI:** Fire lightweight gesture actions mid-swipe once elements reach their final position; commit destructive actions and navigation only when the gesture ends. Users can peek and change their mind halfway without accidentally losing progress. ([freiberg/invisible-details ¶lightweight-actions-such-as])
- **App UI:** Reuse a few gesture metaphors and match each button's animation to its gesture equivalent, so learning one teaches the other. Knowledge compounds, and new gestures are discovered along the path of familiar ones. ([apple/designing-fluid-interfaces ¶because-you-can-discover], [freiberg/invisible-details ¶great-interaction-design-rewards])
- **App UI:** In consumer feed or content apps, make the primary interaction a single, decision-free gesture (e.g. swipe up), with auto-snapping and autoplay removing extra steps. Zero decisions per step keeps cognitive load minimal. *Note: Give autoplaying media a visible pause control, and a tap or button alternative to the gesture.* ([jain/tiktok-ux 1:46])

## Loaders and entrances

- **Marketing:** On marketing pages, use an intro loader (screen reveal, expanding nav, text wipe) only to cover a real heavy-asset load, such as a large background video or 3D scene: hold until the asset is ready (the source holds ~1000ms), slide up over 0.8-1.3s on an exponential curve, then fade the nav in over 200-300ms. It gives the asset time to load; functional loaders otherwise stay short (see also interaction-feel.md § Waits, loading and progress). *Note: Show only on first visit, skip under prefers-reduced-motion, and end as soon as the asset is ready.* ([jain/load-animations-code 6:02], [jain/load-animations-figma 0:01], [jain/trendy-web-animations 0:40])
- **Marketing:** When a loading screen slides away, offset page content slightly down and slide it up concurrently with the same duration and easing. Moving content with the loader makes the page feel fluid. ([jain/load-animations-code 6:58], [jain/load-animations-figma 1:17], [jain/trendy-web-animations 1:34])
- Stagger multiple entering elements slightly rather than animating them in lockstep, e.g. nav items one at a time after their container expands (about 0.2s stagger; fades per the small-element rule in Easing and timing; source: 0.6s fades). Staggered entrances look much better without changing anything else. *Note: In product UI, keep each fade within the under-300ms ease-out rule in Easing and timing and the total stagger short so content isn't held back.* ([jain/awful-to-amazing 6:44], [jain/load-animations-code 16:07])
- **Marketing:** Give decorative elements and key headline words characterful entrances (rotate-pop, fly in, gentle bob, word animations) instead of none or a robotic fade. Text is most of a website and is otherwise neither fun nor interesting. ([jain/boring-design-hacks 3:15])

## Scroll and ambient motion

- **Marketing:** If important images auto-rotate on a timer, turn the active dot into a progress timer and add a slight zoom on hover (for carousel controls, see also components-and-states.md § Cards and carousels). Encourages users to see every image. ([jain/crucial-ui-elements 10:25], [jain/vibe-coding-cant-fix 8:14])
- Use scrolljacking very sparingly, if ever; prefer a well-built hover animation that demonstrates the product over complex scroll animations. Complex scroll animation makes navigation harder and adds little. ([jain/captivating-sections 2:52], [jain/genius-designer 4:20])
- **Marketing:** Keep looping and ambient motion slow and simple: marquees slow enough to read, gentle photo zooms in or out; weigh the usability cost before adding them. Complicated or fast loops are distracting. ([jain/awful-to-amazing 5:32], [jain/trendy-web-animations 4:06])
- **Marketing:** Apply subtle parallax on scroll to elements placed in spacious margins. A lifelike depth effect without busy backgrounds. ([jain/boring-design-hacks 3:48])
